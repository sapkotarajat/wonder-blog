# The Unhinged Reign of Pepy II: How 5 Bizarre Decrees Destroyed an Empire

In 2278 BC, a six-year-old child inherited the wealthiest, most stable superpower on Earth. That child, Pharaoh Pepy II, would go on to rule for an astonishing 94 years—the longest reign in recorded history. While he began his rule as the divine boy-king of the Old Kingdom, his multi-generational tenure ultimately devolved into a masterclass in administrative madness.

History often focuses on the architectural triumphs of Egypt, but the story of Pepy II is a cautionary tale about what happens when absolute power is left unchecked for nearly a century. From using human beings as insect repellent to legalizing his own empire’s bankruptcy, Pepy II’s reign offers a window into how the bizarre, unhinged whims of a single ruler can dismantle a civilization from the inside out. 

If you’ve ever wondered how the glorious age of the pyramid builders suddenly collapsed into a dark age of anarchy, the answer lies in the crumbling sanity of its longest-serving pharaoh.

## 1. The Living Flytrap Mandate: When Comfort Trumps Humanity
Perhaps the most grotesque decree in ancient history was Pepy II’s "Living Flytrap" protocol. The Nile Delta, while fertile, was infamous for its aggressive desert flies—a persistent plague that irritated even the most powerful men in Egypt. While the average citizen relied on clever, practical solutions like mesh netting or burning incense, the pharaoh decided he was above such mundane inconveniences.

Pepy II issued a royal decree requiring several enslaved attendants to be stripped naked and coated from head to toe in thick, sticky honey. These individuals were forced to stand near the pharaoh at all times. The goal? To serve as human bait for the biting insects. 

**Why this matters:**
* **Dehumanization:** The pharaoh viewed these attendants not as humans, but as disposable furniture. 
* **The Psychology of Isolation:** In a court built on absolute, paralyzing flattery, no one dared tell the pharaoh that his hygiene solution was monstrous.
* **The Erosion of Empathy:** When a ruler is treated as a god, they eventually lose the ability to perceive the suffering of those beneath them—a prerequisite for total institutional failure.

## 2. The Pygmy Obsession: State Power for Personal Amusements
Early in his reign, Pepy II displayed an obsessive personality that would later cripple the Egyptian economy. When the explorer Harkhuf sent word that he had acquired a "dwarf of the divine dances" (a pygmy) from the uncharted lands of Yam, the eight-year-old pharaoh lost all sense of governance. 

He didn't care about the ebony, ivory, or frankincense Harkhuf had collected. He bypassed all administrative protocols and fired off an urgent, panicked letter ordering the safe arrival of the dancer. He demanded that ten royal guards inspect the dancer ten times every single night to ensure his safety.

This wasn't just a quirky anecdote; it was a **dangerous precedent.** It signaled to the Egyptian administrative class that state resources—trade fleets, border security, and diplomatic personnel—were secondary to the fleeting whims of the king’s personal amusement. When national priorities become synonymous with a ruler’s hobbies, the state’s foundation begins to crack.

## 3. The Coptos Decrees: Bankrupting the Crown
As Pepy II aged and the pressures of ruling grew, he turned to a disastrous method to maintain political peace: the **Coptos Decrees**. To keep regional priests and powerful local warlords satisfied, he began issuing royal exemptions that stripped the Egyptian central government of its tax base.

These decrees essentially told wealthy provincial temples that they were exempt from all taxes, military conscription, and labor requirements. If a tax collector even stepped onto temple property, they faced the death penalty. 

**The consequence was devastating:**
* **Loss of Revenue:** The central government in Memphis suddenly found its coffers empty.
* **Infrastructure Collapse:** The Nile requires constant, centrally coordinated dredging of irrigation canals. Without tax revenue, the crown could no longer maintain the irrigation systems that fed the people.
* **Famine:** As the central government went bankrupt, provincial temples hoarded grain, leading to mass starvation among the commoners who were once protected by the state.

## 4. The Fiction of Eternal Youth: A Government in Denial
By his eighties and nineties, physical reality was catching up to the pharaoh. He was likely blind, crippled, and suffering from advanced dementia. However, according to Egyptian religious dogma, the pharaoh was the living embodiment of Horus and could not suffer from physical decay.

Instead of appointing a regent or planning a peaceful transition, Pepy II issued a decree mandating that all art and portraiture depict him only as a vibrant, youthful warrior. Scribes and sculptors who dared to represent his true, aged state faced accusations of "cosmic blasphemy." 

This created a **bureaucratic nightmare.** The Egyptian government could not formulate a response to foreign raids or agricultural failure because doing so would require acknowledging that the "divine" pharaoh was, in fact, an incapacitated geriatric. The entire nation was forced to live in a state-mandated delusion, paralyzed by the king’s fear of mortality.

## 5. The Decentralization of Power: Signing the Death Warrant
The final, fatal move in Pepy II’s reign was the decentralization of hereditary titles. In an attempt to stifle local uprisings that resulted from his incompetence, he granted provincial governors (nomarchs) the right to pass their positions to their children. 

Before this, governors were civil servants who could be appointed or removed by the pharaoh. By making these positions hereditary, Pepy II turned his governors into autonomous warlords. They stopped sending taxes to Memphis, began building their own grand tombs, and started raising private armies. 

**The takeaway:** By trying to buy loyalty, he effectively surrendered his sovereignty. When he passed away after his 94-year reign, the centralized administration of Egypt had been hollowed out from within.

## The Aftermath: A Lesson in Leadership
When Pepy II died around 2184 BC, the Old Kingdom—a civilization that had unified Egypt for nearly a thousand years—vanished almost overnight. The result was the **First Intermediate Period**, a 140-year dark age of civil war, famine, and total anarchy.

Archaeologists today look at his crumbling, poorly constructed pyramid as a metaphor for his reign: a structure built on loose rubble that was never destined to last.

### Key Lessons for Modern Life and Leadership:
1. **Beware the "Yes-Men":** If your surroundings are filled only with people who validate your whims, you are likely losing your grip on reality.
2. **Infrastructure Over Optics:** Pepy II spent more energy on his image and his amusements than on the literal canals that watered his people. Never trade long-term stability for short-term reputation management.
3. **Accountability is Mandatory:** No leader should be beyond critique. The moment a leader becomes "infallible" in the eyes of their own administration, the path to collapse has already been paved.

Pepy II remains a powerful reminder that civilizations don't always fall to external invasions; sometimes, they crumble under the weight of an out-of-touch ruler who confuses their own comfort for the good of the people. Success without succession planning, and authority without accountability, is always a recipe for ruin.
