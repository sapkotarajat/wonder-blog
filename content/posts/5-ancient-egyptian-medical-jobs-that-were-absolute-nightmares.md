# 5 Ancient Egyptian Medical Jobs That Were Absolute Nightmares

When you think of Ancient Egypt, your mind likely drifts to golden pharaohs, soaring pyramids, and mysterious mummies. However, beneath the grandeur of the Nile’s greatest civilization lay a world of medicine that was as visceral, brutal, and bizarre as a modern horror film. If you walked into a clinic in 1500 BCE, you wouldn't find sterile white walls or soft-spoken nurses; you would be greeted by the smell of crocodile dung, the sound of rhythmic incantations, and surgeons wielding bronze tools that were more likely to cause an infection than cure one.

For the medical professionals of the era, the job description was a far cry from the high-tech careers of today. From the **"Shepherd of the Anus"** to surgeons attempting brain surgery without anesthesia, these individuals operated in a landscape where life and death were separated by a thin, blood-stained linen cloth. In this post, we’re peeling back the bandages to explore five of the most harrowing jobs in ancient history.

## 1. The 'Shepherd of the Anus' (Nrt Phwt)
In the royal court of 2500 BCE, you might assume the most prestigious role would involve guarding the Pharaoh’s treasury or advising on military strategy. In reality, one of the most respected positions was the *Nrt Phwt*, or the "Shepherd of the Anus." 

This wasn’t a low-level custodial role; it was a high-ranking medical specialty. Egyptians believed that the bowel was the center of both life and death. They suffered from a pervasive fear of *whdw*—decaying matter—which they believed produced toxic gases that traveled upward to the brain, causing everything from madness to physical decay. 

**The daily grind of a Shepherd of the Anus:**
*   **The Ritual Enema:** Using hollowed-out cow horns or reeds, these specialists manually flushed the royal digestive tract with mixtures of milk, water, and medicinal oils. 
*   **Constant Vigilance:** The physician was expected to perform daily inspections of the Pharaoh’s stool to ensure no "decay" was accumulating.
*   **Manual Intervention:** Given the lack of modern medical equipment, these interventions were invasive, intimate, and physically demanding. 

It was a career defined by its proximity to the human body’s most "unmentionable" parts, all in the service of keeping the "living god" of Egypt from succumbing to his own internal waste.

## 2. The Ancient Eye Specialist (Neru)
Living in the desert meant constant exposure to blinding sun, airborne sand, and relentless dust. Consequently, eye infections like trachoma were a plague that affected almost every citizen. Enter the *Neru*, the ancient Egyptian ophthalmologist. 

Because there was no such thing as anesthesia, "surgery" was an exercise in pure survival. These doctors used sharpened bronze needles to scrape away infections or attempt to move cataracts. Patients were often held down by several burly assistants while the doctor poked around the eyeball with a metal spike. 

**The Bizarre Treatment Protocols:**
Perhaps the most terrifying aspect was the prescription. According to the *Edwin Smith Papyrus*, a common remedy for blindness involved:
1.  Taking a human brain and dividing it into two halves.
2.  Mixing one half with honey for an evening application.
3.  Drying the other half for a morning application.

The mental toll of sourcing these ingredients—often requiring the surgeon to act as a *de facto* graverobber—is difficult for the modern mind to fathom. For the *Neru*, every procedure was a high-stakes gamble: one slip of the hand meant permanent blindness, a social disgrace, and potentially the wrath of a grieving family.

## 3. The Dentist: Battling the 'Tooth Worm'
Hesy-Ra, who lived around 2600 BCE, holds the title of the first named dentist in human history. His world was one of agony and superstition. The Egyptians were convinced that dental pain was caused by microscopic "tooth worms" that burrowed into the jaw. 

To "release" these imaginary parasites, dentists would use a **bronze drill** to bore deep into the patient’s jawbone to drain abscesses. Without painkillers (aside from a bit of beer or wine), the treatment was often as traumatic as the disease.

*   **Prosthetic Innovation:** When a tooth was lost, they didn't have implants. They would carve a replacement out of ivory or harvest one from a deceased person or a slave, then wire it into place using gold or silver thread.
*   **The Infection Risk:** Imagine the agony of having your loose teeth wired to your inflamed gums in a dark, humid room, all while the metal taste of oxidized copper filled your mouth. It was a brutal test of endurance for both the dentist and the patient.

## 4. The Pharmacist: The Master of Waste
In modern times, a pharmacist is a chemist in a white coat. In Ancient Egypt, the "Preparer of Remedies" was essentially a handler of biological waste. To understand the Egyptian pharmacopeia, you have to realize that they believed in the medicinal power of the grotesque. 

**Common (and questionable) pharmacy ingredients:**
*   **Crocodile Dung:** Used as a contraceptive, likely because its high alkalinity could serve as a rudimentary spermicide (though the method of harvesting it was a death trap).
*   **Fly Droppings:** Frequently mixed with milk as a treatment for crying babies.
*   **Animal Organs:** A vast array of lizard and bird parts were ground into pastes by hand.

The pharmacist lived in a perpetual state of olfactory assault and health risk. These professionals were effectively the first people to handle high-level biohazards without the benefit of gloves, masks, or even a basic understanding of germ theory. They weren't just chemists; they were big-game hunters of the Nile, braving swampy waters just to gather the "raw materials" for a royal headache cure.

## 5. The Trauma Surgeon: Life on the Front Lines
Whether they were stationed with the army or overseeing the construction of the Great Pyramids, the Trauma Surgeon was the "first responder" of the ancient world. Their most notorious practice was **trepanning**—the act of drilling holes into the human skull to relieve pressure from head wounds. 

Archaeological evidence shows that patients occasionally survived, but the surgeon's life was a constant, high-pressure nightmare. 

**Why it was such a brutal profession:**
*   **The Tools:** They used obsidian scalpels. While sharper than modern steel, obsidian is glass—and therefore incredibly brittle. If a blade shattered during a craniotomy, the patient was doomed.
*   **The Environment:** They performed complex procedures in the middle of desert heat, surrounded by dust and unsterilized conditions.
*   **Magico-Religious Duties:** Beyond the physical surgery, they had to perform intense incantations to the goddess Sekhmet. If the patient died, it wasn't just a surgical failure; it was a spiritual one. The doctor was often blamed for offending the gods, creating a massive psychological burden.

## The Legacy of the "Swnw"
It is easy to look back at these practices—the brain-paste, the crocodile dung, and the tooth worms—and dismiss them as the superstitions of a primitive culture. However, the *Swnw* (general physicians) were the pioneers of modern medicine. They were the first to record medical cases in documents like the *Ebers Papyrus*, and they were the first to move away from pure magic toward **observation and diagnosis.**

They categorized ailments into three logical groups:
1.  **Ailments to be treated:** Minor issues they could fix.
2.  **Ailments to be contended with:** Chronic or difficult conditions.
3.  **Ailments not to be treated:** The first true system of medical triage.

### What Can We Learn from These Nightmares?
The next time you’re sitting in a sterile, air-conditioned doctor’s office, take a moment to appreciate the advancements of the last 4,000 years. The doctors of Ancient Egypt were dealing with the same human frailties—broken bones, infections, and disease—but they were doing it with almost no resources and an immense amount of social pressure.

They were "brave, and perhaps slightly crazy," individuals who dared to look inside the human body when everyone else was too afraid to look. They were the unsung heroes of history, working in the most "nightmarish" conditions imaginable to forge the path that eventually led us to modern, life-saving medicine.

**The big takeaway?** Never complain about your job again—unless, of course, your current boss asks you to harvest crocodile dung.
